## 4.3 Price Forecasts Against Actual Prices

The error metrics in Section 4.2 summarise how far each forecast sits from the outcome, but they do not show what each forecast looks like next to the price that actually settled. This section compares the forecasts with the actual half hourly prices directly, by comparing their price levels, their daily averages, their ability to flag high price intervals, and their placement of the daily peak. The window is the same 181 forecast days and 8,679 periods used in Section 4.2.

Table [10] sets the distribution of each forecast against the distribution of actual prices. The actual price averaged 74.3 A$/MWh over the window, with a median of 69.6 A$/MWh, a 95th percentile of 136.3 A$/MWh, and a maximum of 10,047 A$/MWh on 10 January.

| Series | Mean | Median | P95 | Max | Bias vs actual | Median abs. error | Within ±20 A$/MWh | Rank corr. with actual |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Actual | 74.3 | 69.6 | 136.3 | 10,047.0 | | | | |
| Seasonal naive | 73.8 | 69.2 | 136.0 | 10,047.0 | −0.4 | 17.2 | 56.1% | 0.69 |
| ARIMA(0,1,5) | 75.1 | 67.9 | 122.1 | 382.1 | +0.8 | 22.2 | 46.0% | 0.38 |
| Hybrid | 81.1 | 73.1 | 223.9 | 10,207.9 | +6.9 | 39.3 | 29.0% | 0.17 |
| AEMO pre-dispatch | 77.0 | 68.8 | 134.9 | 12,571.5 | +2.7 | 7.5 | 79.3% | 0.87 |

Values are in A$/MWh. Rank correlation is Spearman's, which is used because a handful of extreme intervals dominate the Pearson coefficient.

The seasonal naive and ARIMA forecasts reproduce the average level of the market closely, with a bias of less than 1 A$/MWh, but they reproduce it in different ways. The naive forecast has almost exactly the same distribution as the actual price because it is the actual price shifted by one week, and a typical interval sits 17.2 A$/MWh from the outcome. ARIMA has the right mean but a much narrower spread, with a maximum forecast of 382 A$/MWh against an actual maximum above 10,000 A$/MWh, which explains why it records the lowest RMSE of the project's models in Table [6] while ranking intervals poorly (rank correlation of 0.38). The hybrid overstates the average price by 6.9 A$/MWh, carries a 95th percentile of 224 A$/MWh against an actual 136 A$/MWh, and produces forecasts as low as −252 A$/MWh where the actual minimum was −50 A$/MWh. Its typical interval error of 39.3 A$/MWh is more than half the average price, and only 29% of its forecasts fall within 20 A$/MWh of the actual price, against 56% for the naive base it corrects. Pre-dispatch has both the closest typical interval (7.5 A$/MWh) and the highest rank correlation (0.87).

Table [11] compares the mean actual price in each month with the mean of each forecast, which shows the level offset described in Section 4.2 directly.

| Month | Actual | Seasonal naive | ARIMA(0,1,5) | Hybrid | AEMO pre-dispatch |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| January | 67.3 | 66.0 | 70.0 | 98.9 | 82.9 |
| February | 83.8 | 85.3 | 88.1 | 119.2 | 93.2 |
| March | 70.4 | 69.8 | 70.5 | 98.2 | 66.5 |
| April | 59.7 | 59.7 | 61.0 | 61.2 | 57.1 |
| May | 82.0 | 82.2 | 82.8 | 65.6 | 80.3 |
| June | 83.3 | 80.8 | 78.8 | 45.8 | 82.9 |

The naive and ARIMA monthly means are within 4.5 A$/MWh of the actual in every month. The hybrid is 28 to 35 A$/MWh above the actual in January to March, close to it in April, and 16 and 38 A$/MWh below it in May and June, so the hybrid predicted June prices to be about half of what they were. This pattern matches a residual correction trained on an earlier price regime and applied as a near constant offset after the regime changed.

The same picture holds at the daily level, which is the horizon at which a day ahead forecast is most often used. Table [12] compares the forecast daily average price with the actual daily average, and Figure [9] plots both across the window.

| Model | MAE of daily mean (A$/MWh) | Bias (A$/MWh) | Days within ±20 A$/MWh | Days within ±10% | Rank corr. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seasonal naive | 22.7 | −0.4 | 71.8% | 28.7% | 0.67 |
| ARIMA(0,1,5) | 22.9 | +0.8 | 70.7% | 26.5% | 0.59 |
| Hybrid | 53.6 | +7.1 | 43.1% | 15.5% | 0.03 |
| AEMO pre-dispatch | 9.3 | +2.7 | 97.8% | 83.4% | 0.98 |

The naive and ARIMA forecasts place the daily average within 20 A$/MWh of the actual on about 7 days in 10, while the hybrid does so on 4 days in 10. The hybrid's rank correlation of 0.03 with the actual daily average means its forecasts carry almost no information about which days will be expensive and which will be cheap, which is a stronger statement of the deficit than the RMSE in Table [6]. Pre-dispatch places the daily average within 20 A$/MWh on 98% of days, but as noted in Section 4.1 the archived series is the final pre-dispatch run, so this comparison favours it.

Table [13] treats each forecast as a warning of high prices by counting the intervals in which the actual price exceeded 130 A$/MWh, the fixed spike threshold of Section 3.3, and checking whether the forecast for that interval also exceeded it. The count of 538 intervals across 58 days differs from the 562 spike periods in Table [6], because the spike segment also includes intervals flagged by the variable threshold.

| Model | Intervals forecast above 130 | Hits | Misses | False alarms | Hit rate | Precision | Spike days flagged (of 58) | False spike days |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seasonal naive | 525 | 186 | 352 | 339 | 34.6% | 35.4% | 34 | 23 |
| ARIMA(0,1,5) | 384 | 57 | 481 | 327 | 10.6% | 14.8% | 7 | 4 |
| Hybrid | 1,690 | 129 | 409 | 1,561 | 24.0% | 7.6% | 35 | 61 |
| AEMO pre-dispatch | 514 | 366 | 172 | 148 | 68.0% | 71.2% | 54 | 7 |

The hybrid forecasts a price above 130 A$/MWh in 1,690 intervals, more than three times as often as the price actually exceeded it, and 92% of those forecasts are false alarms. It still catches fewer actual high price intervals than the naive base (129 against 186), so the LSTM correction raises the number of warnings while lowering the number of correct ones. At the daily level the hybrid flags 35 of the 58 days that contained a high price interval, but it also flags 61 days that did not. ARIMA rarely forecasts high prices in the right interval and flags only 7 of the 58 days. Pre-dispatch catches two thirds of the high price intervals with a precision above 70%.

Table [14] compares the daily maximum price, which is the quantity a market participant exposed to price spikes cares most about. The mean actual daily maximum over the window was 235.1 A$/MWh.

| Model | Mean forecast daily max (A$/MWh) | Median abs. error of daily max (A$/MWh) | Peak within 1 hour of actual peak | Median peak timing error (hours) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seasonal naive | 234.7 | 20.8 | 30.4% | 6.5 |
| ARIMA(0,1,5) | 82.9 | 34.9 | 3.3% | 15.5 |
| Hybrid | 266.2 | 48.0 | 20.4% | 8.5 |
| AEMO pre-dispatch | 319.9 | 6.0 | 55.2% | 1.0 |

ARIMA forecasts a mean daily maximum of 82.9 A$/MWh against an actual 235.1 A$/MWh and places the peak within an hour of the actual peak on only 3% of days, because its forecast is nearly flat within the day. The hybrid's daily maximum is on average 31 A$/MWh too high, its typical error on the daily maximum is more than double the naive base's, and it times the peak correctly less often than the naive base (20% of days against 30%). Pre-dispatch places the peak within an hour on 55% of days, with a median timing error of one hour.

Figures [10] and [11] overlay the four forecasts on the actual price for a calm week (6 to 12 April) and a spike week (2 to 8 February), and Figure [12] plots each forecast against the actual price for every interval between −50 and 400 A$/MWh. In the calm week the naive, hybrid, and pre-dispatch forecasts all follow the daily shape of the actual price, while ARIMA holds a flat line between 55 and 85 A$/MWh. The hybrid follows the naive shape but overshoots the evening peaks by 30 to 80 A$/MWh and undershoots the midday troughs, which is consistent with Table [10].

Taken together, the direct comparisons with actual prices confirm the ranking from Section 4.2 and add detail the error metrics conceal. ARIMA's low RMSE comes from a flat forecast near the average price that rarely identifies when prices will be high, and the hybrid's deficit comes from a level offset that moves the whole forecast away from the actual price and adds a large number of false high price warnings.

The actual price on 13 October contained no extreme interval. It averaged 244.3 A$/MWh, peaked at 348.6 A$/MWh at 12:30, and stayed between 179 and 318 A$/MWh from 14:00 to 19:30, across both the afternoon and evening windows in which the forecasters had placed scarcity. The hybrid's copied afternoon spike of 4,475 to 13,109 A$/MWh therefore produced errors of up to 12,800 A$/MWh, and its daily average forecast of 722.9 A$/MWh was almost three times the actual. Pre-dispatch's evening values of 20,111 A$/MWh produced errors of up to 19,800 A$/MWh and a daily average forecast of 1,517.3 A$/MWh, more than six times the actual. ARIMA, which stayed between 32 and 171 A$/MWh, sat below the actual price all day and recorded the lowest RMSE of the three at 170.7 A$/MWh, while its MAE of 155.9 A$/MWh reflects that it underestimated a day that was expensive throughout. Outside the scarcity windows the hybrid was the closest of the three, with a median absolute error of 78.2 A$/MWh against 109.4 A$/MWh for pre-dispatch and 158.1 A$/MWh for ARIMA.

The episode supports the conclusion that neither forecaster anticipated the market correctly. A naive based forecast that copies yesterday's extreme interval is penalised heavily when the event does not recur, and pre-dispatch issued the evening before also forecast scarcity that did not eventuate, so both forecasts issued the evening before carried large errors when the extreme intervals did not occur. On a single scored extreme day this is a descriptive observation rather than a statistical result.
